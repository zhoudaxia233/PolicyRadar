COMMISSION IMPLEMENTING DECISION (EU) 2026/2260

of 8 October 2026

laying down rules for the application of Directive 2010/75/EU of the European Parliament and of the Council as regards uniform conditions for operating rules for rearing of poultry and pigs

(notified under document C(2026) 6899)

(Text with EEA relevance)

THE EUROPEAN COMMISSION,

Having regard to the Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union,

Having regard to Directive 2010/75/EU of the European Parliament and of the Council of 24 November 2010 on industrial and livestock rearing emissions (integrated pollution prevention and control) (1) , and in particular Article 70i(2), second subparagraph, thereof,

Whereas:

(1)

Directive 2010/75/EU lays down rules to prevent or reduce pollution arising from industrial activities. In this context, the Commission is to establish uniform conditions for operating rules for the activities referred to in Annex Ia to that Directive, namely the rearing of pigs and poultry of a certain capacity.

(2)

A sub-group to the Industrial Emissions Expert Group has been created to support the Commission in the early preparation of the implementing act establishing the uniform conditions for operating rules for poultry and pigs rearing in accordance with Article 70i IED, before submission to the committee in accordance with Regulation (EU) No 182/2011 of the European Parliament and of the Council (2). The sub-group fulfils the call on the Commission, pursuant to Article 70i(1) of the IED to carry out appropriate consultations and organise an exchange of information during the preparatory work of these uniform conditions, including at expert level.

(3)

The sub-group met on several occasions from February 2025 until May 2026. In this context, measures concerning on-farm processes and activities, such as nutritional management, feed preparation, housing, manure management, including collection, storage, processing and land spreading, as well as storage of dead animals were discussed. The sub-group provided data and shared information, based on which the uniform conditions for operating rules have been developed.

(4)

The economic feasibility of certain techniques varies depending on the farm size. Consequently, it is appropriate to set proportionate requirements for farms carrying out activities referred to in Annex Ia to Directive 2010/75/EU which did not fall within the scope of that Directive before 4 August 2024.

(5)

In order to allow for the most cost-effective techniques to be employed at farm level, the best-available techniques and emerging techniques referred to in the uniform conditions for operating rules should be neither prescriptive nor exhaustive. It should be possible to use other techniques that ensure at least an equivalent level of environmental protection, including human health and climate protection. Therefore, in the event that techniques not listed in the uniform conditions are applied, it is necessary that Member States validate that their use ensures at least an equivalent level of environmental protection, based on scientifically established methods, also taking into account animal welfare systems recognised at Union, national or international level.

(6)

Information on the environmental performance of the techniques listed in the uniform conditions for operating rules (e.g. efficiency for ammonia emissions reduction) is based on the information exchange pursuant to Article 70i(1) of Directive 2010/75/EU, and on recognised international guidance (e.g. the UNECE guidance document on ‘Options for ammonia mitigation’ (3)). It should be possible to use additional information on the environmental performance of the techniques from Union, national or international sources that ensure the provision of data of an equivalent scientific quality. When the use of additional information leads to different environmental performance than the one listed in the uniform conditions, or when the environmental performance of the techniques is not specified in the uniform conditions, it is necessary that Member States, as appropriate, validate the environmental performance of the techniques, based on scientifically established methods.

(7)

It is also necessary that Member States validate the environmental performance of combinations of techniques, based on scientifically established methods, and that Member States ensure that all underlying data, methodologies, and assumptions are fully documented and publicly available.

(8)

To reduce the administrative burden involved in the application of techniques not listed in the uniform conditions for operating rules, Member States should strive to cooperate in order to foster mutual recognition of techniques.

(9)

The maximum values of ammonia emissions to air established in these uniform conditions for operating rules should be understood as setting emission limit values as referred to in Article 70d(2) and Article 70f(1) of the directive.

(10)

The maximum values of total nitrogen excreted and maximum values of total phosphorus excreted established in the uniform conditions for operating rules should be understood as setting environmental performance limit values as referred to in Article 70d(2) and Article 70f(1) of Directive 2010/75/EU.

(11)

Article 3(5) of Directive (EU) 2024/1785 of the European Parliament and of the Council (4) sets out that in relation to installations carrying out activities referred to in Annex Ia to Directive 2010/75/EU, Member States are required to apply the laws, regulations and administrative provisions adopted in accordance with Directive (EU) 2024/1785 within: (a) four years of the entry into force of the implementing act referred to in Article 70i(2) of Directive 2010/75/EU, if the installation has a capacity of 600 livestock units (LSU) or more; (b) five years of the entry into force of the implementing act referred to in Article 70i(2) of Directive 2010/75/EU, if the installation has a capacity of 400 LSU or more; (c) six years of the entry into force of the implementing act referred to in Article 70i(2) of Directive 2010/75/EU, for all other installations covered by Annex Ia to Directive 2010/75/EU. Until the relevant date of application, as referred to in Article 3(5), first subparagraph, of Directive (EU) 2024/1785, the installations mentioned in that subparagraph which fall within the scope of Directive 2010/75/EU in the version in force on the day before Directive (EU) 2024/1785 enters into force are required to comply with Directive 2010/75/EU in that version. It is therefore appropriate, for the purpose of applying the uniform conditions for operating rules, that a ‘new farm’ is defined as a farm whose construction has been authorised or registered after the applicable deadline set out in Article 3(5) of Directive (EU) 2024/1785, or as a complete replacement of a farm after the applicable deadline set out in Article 3(5) of Directive (EU) 2024/1785.

(12)

The maximum values of ammonia emissions to air from animal housing should be set at the level of each animal house present at the farm. However, Member States should be given the option to apply maximum values across all animal houses at the same farm, provided that there is no net increase in ammonia emissions from housing in the farm, as this would enable the implementation of cost-effective measures by farmers.

(13)

As an alternative to the maximum values of ammonia emissions to air set out in the uniform conditions, Member States should be allowed to use a relative system based on minimum ammonia reduction requirements at each animal house as compared to a reference system based on Union or international sources (e.g. UNECE guidance document on ‘Options for ammonia mitigation’ (5)), provided that it ensures at least the same reduction of ammonia emissions and that it is based on scientifically established methods recognised at Union or international levels.

(14)

Emission factors associated to certain animal categories and techniques, or the environmental performance of techniques may change over time due to new scientific findings. In such cases, where the change no longer allows compliance with the maximum values set out in the uniform conditions for operating rules, Member States should ensure that farmers who implemented techniques that were enabling compliance before the change occurred are not penalised. However, Member States should take the necessary measures to assist farmers to re-establish compliance.

(15)

Weaners should be defined as young pigs reared from weaning until fattening, typically reared from a live weight of around 8 kg until a live weight of around 30 kg. However, the emissions associated with weaners increase as their live weight increases. Therefore, for the purpose of calculating the installation capacity, conversion rates that reflect that increase in emissions over time have been included in the Commission’s proposal for a Directive of the European Parliament and of the Council amending Directive (EU) 2024/1785 (6), by differentiating between weaners below and above 20 kg of live weight and by ensuring that weaners above 20 kg of live weight are accounted for as ‘other pigs’ for the purpose of calculating the installation capacity.

(16)

Pursuant to Article 70c(5) of Directive 2010/75/EU, the Commission is to assess the impacts of the implementation of the operating rules laid down in Article 70i of that Directive and submit, by 11 years after the entry into force of the implementing act referred to in Article 70i(2) of that Directive, a report to the European Parliament and to the Council on the results of that assessment.

(17)

In order to ensure that the uniform conditions for operating rules reflect scientific and technological developments, the Commission should ensure their periodic reviews and updates. Such updates should aim at integrating developments in best-available techniques, emerging techniques, and evolving environmental and animal welfare standards. Such updates should also take into account the findings of the Innovation centre for industrial transformation and emissions.

(18)

The Commission should update this Decision where developments in the best-available techniques allow for a significant reduction of emissions or following the assessment referred to in Article 70c(5) of Directive 2010/75/EU, where relevant.

(19)

Pursuant to Article 70i(3) of Directive 2010/75/EU, Member States are to ensure that the competent authority follows or is informed of developments in best-available techniques and of the publication of any new or updated uniform conditions for operating rules.

(20)

It is appropriate to implement targeted flexible requirements for production systems improving animal welfare recognised at Union, national or international level, which may, in certain cases, lead to higher emissions than conventional systems.

(21)

In its Communication ‘Vision for Agriculture and Food’ (7), the Commission highlighted the need for a future-proof agri-food sector that functions within planetary boundaries, where farming and the food sector contribute together to the Union’s climate objectives, while preserving healthy soils, clean water and air, and protecting and restoring Union’s biodiversity. The Commission has also set the target of cutting administrative burden and meaningful simplification in policy areas that affect farmers.

(22)

The measures provided for in this Decision are in accordance with the opinion of the Committee referred to in Article 75 of Directive 2010/75/EU,

HAS ADOPTED THIS DECISION:

Article 1

The uniform conditions for operating rules as referred to in Article 70i of Directive 2010/75/EU are those set out in the Annex to this Decision.

Article 2

1.   Other techniques than those listed in the Annex to this Decision may be used. In such cases, Member States shall validate, based on scientifically established methods, that the use of such techniques, as defined in Article 3, point (10)(a), of Directive 2010/75/EU ensures at least an equivalent level of environmental protection to those listed in the Annex, including human health and climate protection, also taking into account animal welfare systems recognised at Union, national or international level.

2.   Environmental performances of techniques listed in the Annex to this Decision that are different from those listed in Sections 4 and 5 of that Annex may be used. In such cases, Member States shall validate such environmental performances, based on scientifically established methods.

3.   When techniques listed in the Annex to this Decision are used for which the environmental performance is not specified in Sections 4 and 5 of that Annex, Member States shall validate their environmental performance.

4.   When combinations of techniques are used, Member States shall validate the environmental performance of such combinations of techniques.

5.   Member States shall ensure that all underlying data required by Directive 2003/4/EC of the European Parliament and of the Council (8), methodologies, and assumptions used for the validations carried out in accordance with paragraphs 1 to 4 of this Article, are fully documented and publicly available.

Article 3

1.   Member States may decide to use, as an alternative to the maximum values of ammonia emissions to air set out in the Annex to this Decision, a relative system based on minimum ammonia reduction requirements at each animal house as compared to a reference system based on Union or international sources. In this case Member States shall ensure that such system provides at least the same reduction of ammonia emissions as the one achieved when using the maximum values of ammonia emissions to air set out in that Annex, and that it is based on scientifically established methods.

2.   Member States referred to in paragraph 1 shall ensure that all underlying data, required by Directive 2003/4/EC, methodologies, and assumptions are fully documented and publicly available via the internet. They shall inform the Commission about the use of the alternative approach and about the underlying data, methodologies and assumptions.

3.   Member States that decide, as an alternative to the maximum values of ammonia emissions to air at each animal house, to apply maximum values of ammonia emissions to air across all animal houses at the same farm, shall ensure that there is no net increase in ammonia emissions from housing in the farm.

Article 4

This Decision shall apply as follows:

(a)

not later than 4 years from 8 October 2026 for installations covered by Annex Ia of Directive 2010/75/EU with a capacity of 600 LSU or more;

(b)

not later than 5 years from 8 October 2026 for installations covered by Annex Ia of Directive 2010/75/EU with a capacity of 400 LSU or more;

(c)

not later than 6 years from 8 October 2026 for all other installations covered by Annex Ia of Directive 2010/75/EU.

Article 5

This Decision is addressed to the Member States.

Done at Brussels, 8 October 2026.

ANNEX

UNIFORM CONDITIONS FOR OPERATING RULES FOR LIVESTOCK (PIGS AND POULTRY REARING)

SCOPE

These Uniform Conditions (UC) concern the activities specified in Annex Ia to Directive 2010/75/EU (the IED).

The Livestock Unit (LSU) level of an installation is to be calculated using the conversion rates given in Annex Ia to the IED.

In particular, the UC cover the following on-farm processes and activities:

(a)

nutritional management;

(b)

feed preparation;

(c)

housing;

(d)

manure management, including collection, storage, processing and landspreading;

(e)

storage of dead animals.

The UC apply in parallel to other relevant legislation, e.g. Council Directive 91/676/EEC (1), Directive (EU) 2016/2284 of the European Parliament and of the Council (2), Regulation (EC) No 1069/2009 of the European Parliament and of the Council (3), legislation on animal welfare (e.g. Council Directive 2008/120/EC (4), Council Directive 98/58/EC (5), Council Directive 1999/74/EC (6), Council Directive 2007/43/EC (7)).

Term used

Space provided per animal in a housing system taking into account the maximum capacity of the plant.

Any method of soil cultivation that leaves the previous year's crop residue (such as corn stalks or wheat stubble) on the surface of fields before and after planting the next crop, to reduce soil erosion and run-off.

Manure

Newly covered farm

An existing farm carrying out activities referred to in Annex Ia and which did not fall in the scope of the IED before 4 August 2024.

A plant whose construction at the site of the farm has been authorised or registered after the applicable deadline set out in Article 3(5) of Directive (EU) 2024/1785 or a complete replacement of a plant on the existing foundations, after the applicable deadline set out in Article 3(5) of that Directive.

On-farm

Within the premises of the IED installation.

Organic production defined in accordance with Regulation (EU) 2018/848

Plant

A part of the farm where one of the following processes or activities is carried out: animal housing, manure storage, manure processing. A plant consists of a single building (or facility) and/or the necessary equipment to carry out processes or activities.

Sensitive receptor

Areas which need special protection, such as:

(a)

residential areas;

(c)

sensitive ecosystems/habitats.

Faeces and urine mixed or not with some litter material and some water to give a liquid manure that flows under gravity and can be pumped.

Slurry stores

Fixed structures designed to hold slurry.

Solid manure

Faeces or droppings and urine mixed or not with litter material that do not flow under gravity and cannot be pumped.

Waste water

Rainwater run-off commonly mixed with manure, water derived from the cleaning of surfaces (e.g. floors) and equipment, and water derived from the operation of air cleaning systems. GENERAL CONSIDERATIONS

These Uniform Conditions for operating rules are consistent with the use of best available techniques for the activities listed in Annex Ia to the IED. This document also includes information on emerging techniques, in accordance with Article 70i of the IED.

The best available techniques and emerging techniques in this document are neither prescriptive nor exhaustive. Other techniques may be used that ensure at least an equivalent level of environmental protection, including human health and climate protection.

Information on the environmental performance of the techniques listed in Section 4 and Section 5 (e.g. efficiency for ammonia emissions reduction) is based on the information exchange pursuant to Article 70i of the IED, and on recognised international guidance (e.g. UNECE guidance document on ‘Options for ammonia mitigation’). Additional information on the environmental performance of the techniques or their combinations may be taken from Union, national or international sources (e.g. UNECE guidance documents or EMEP/EEA air pollutant emission inventory guidebook) that ensure the provision of data of an equivalent scientific quality.

It is for Member States to validate, based on scientifically established methods:

(a)

the use of techniques not listed in this document that ensure at least an equivalent level of environmental protection, including human health and climate protection, also taking into account animal welfare systems recognised at Union, national or international level;

(b)

the use of environmental performance of techniques listed in this document that are different to those in Sections 4 and 5 or the environmental performance where not specified in Sections 4 and 5;

(c)

the environmental performance of combinations of techniques.

All underlying data, required by Directive 2003/4/EC of the European Parliament and of the Council (8), methodologies, and assumptions, must be fully documented and publicly available.

Frequent slurry removal, e.g. by vacuum system, scraper, flushing (in the case of a fully or partly slatted floor)

Vacuum system: outlets at the bottom of the pit or channel are connected to a discharge pipe underneath which transfers slurry to outdoor storage. Slurry is frequently discharged by opening a valve or a plug in the main slurry pipe, e.g. once or twice every week; a slight vacuum develops and allows the complete emptying of the pit or channel. A certain depth of slurry needs to be obtained before the vacuum can be effective and the system can operate properly. A vacuum pump is not necessary if the slurry is flushed by gravity to a tank located on a level lower than the barn. From this tank the slurry can be pumped to a storage tank or to a mobile tanker.

Vacuum system: Ammonia reductions of at least 25 % (for removal at least twice per week) and 15 % (for removal at least every two weeks) can be achieved in comparison to a “deep pit with fully slatted floor without any additional techniques” reference system. Methane emissions are also reduced.

Scraper: there is a V-shaped channel with two inclined surfaces on each side of a central gutter, where urine can be drained to a collection pit through a drain in the bottom of the manure channel. From the pit, the solid fraction of the manure is extracted frequently (e.g. up to 12 times a day) by a scraper. The addition of a coating on the scraped floor is recommended in order to achieve a smooth(er) surface.

This technique can be combined with individual variations of the bottoms of channels or pits, e.g. gutters, tubes or a permanent slurry layer.

Flushing: An ammonia reduction of at least 25 % can be achieved in comparison to a “deep pit with fully slatted floor without any additional techniques” reference system.

For slanted walls: the manure channel creates a V section with the point of discharge at the bottom. Manure removal is carried out at least twice every week.

The slope and the smoothness of the surface facilitate the slurry discharge.

An ammonia reduction of at least 40 % can be achieved in comparison to a “deep pit with fully slatted floor without any additional techniques” reference system. Methane emissions are also reduced.

Reduced manure pit (in the case of partly slatted floor)

The pen is equipped with a narrow pit with a width of about 0,6 m. The pit can be located in an external alley.

An ammonia reduction of at least 30 % can be achieved in comparison to a “deep pit with fully slatted floor without any additional techniques” reference system.

e

Full litter system (in the case of a solid concrete floor)

A fully concrete floor almost completely covered with a layer of straw or other lignocellulosic material.

In the litter-floored system, solid manure is frequently removed (e.g. twice per week). Alternatively in the deep litter system, fresh straw is added on top and the accumulated manure is removed at the end of the rearing cycle. In order to include animal welfare considerations, separate functional areas can be organised into lying, feeding, walking and defecating areas.

The lying area (about 50 to60 % of the total area) consists of a levelled insulated concrete floor with covered, insulated huts or kennels, with a hinged roof that can be raised or lowered to control temperature and ventilation. The activity and feeding areas have a slatted floor with a manure pit underneath and frequent manure removal, e.g. by vacuum. Straw can be used on the solid concrete floor.

A reduction of the slurry temperature (usually to reach 15°C) is achieved by installing a cooling system placed above the slurry (surface cooling with fins), above the concrete floor or cast into the floor (bottom cooling). Acid (e.g. sulphuric acid) is added to slurry in order to lower the pH to about 5,5 in the slurry pit. The addition can be carried out in a process tank, followed by aeration and homogenisation. Part of the treated slurry is pumped back to the storage pit under the housing floors. The treatment system is fully automated. Prior to (or after) landspreading on acid soils, lime addition may be required to neutralise the pH of the soil. Alternatively, acidification can be performed directly in the slurry store or continuously during landspreading.

t

Housing with distinct living zones for animals

Animals are raised in groups, in housing systems comprising at least three functional zones: lying/resting, feeding/activity and excretion. The surface area per pig is larger in comparison to conventional housing systems. An outdoor yard may also be available, where animals have access to fresh air and natural light, thus improving animal welfare. By having different functional zones, the pollutant characteristics of each zone can be targeted via specific techniques. For instance, the excretion zone can be in the outdoor yard with a small emitting surface and frequent manure removal (e.g. up to 12 times a day using a V-shaped scraper with a urine gutter / underfloor faeces-urine segregation). Normally, the animals keep the lying and feeding area clean (this natural behaviour is enhanced by natural/hybrid ventilation for temperature differentiation between the excretion zone and the other zones). In comparison to conventional systems, the consumption of water per pig is typically higher (due to the greater surface area per pig), while the energy consumption is lower (due to natural/hybrid ventilation). The labour requirements are also typically higher (e.g due to cleaning of straw from a larger area).

An ammonia reduction of at least 75 % can be achieved in comparison to a “deep pit with fully slatted floor without any additional techniques” reference system.

u

Use of the following additives for the treatment of manure:

(a)

ion-exchange-based materials;

(b)

selected microorganisms.

Additives aimed at changing the physical, chemical and biological properties of slurry can be placed, manually or mechanically, on emitting (fouled) surfaces (e.g. slats, paved /solid floor, below slats), in the pits or in the manure storage facilities. The initial application is followed by periodic additions at varying frequency (determined by the active principle of each additive). Slurry additives include several categories, for example, complexes of bacteria or microorganisms, zeolites and chemical products. They act, for example, by changing the natural microbial balance, by inducing bio-acidification (production of organic acids) which lowers pH (thus reducing ammonia volatilisation), by limiting biological pathways leading to sulphide formation. This results in decreasing, for instance, ammonia, methane, hydrogen sulphide and/or odour emissions. In addition to the emission reduction, the additives can improve slurry handling by inducing liquefaction (lower viscosity) and/or crust weakening/breakdown (resulting in better pumpability and less foaming), thus reducing agitation needs, protecting equipment and limiting manual interventions (including those associated with hazardous H2S exposure).

—

Ion-exchange-based materials (natural zeolites, activated zeolites or clay minerals) remove ammonia from slurry by trapping positive ions from solutions (NH4 +) and replacing them with exchangeable positive ions (Na+, Ca2+) which are contained in the porous surfaces of the mineral crystalline structure.

—

Authorised microorganisms in the EFSA QPS (qualified presumption of safety) list (e.g. lactic acid bacteria) achieve conservation and stabilisation of livestock manure by spraying (liquid suspension) or dispersing (combination with drying powder). This can be done in the different stages of livestock rearing and production: on bedding/litter, in the pit (above/below), manure storage, on-farm processing of manure (e.g. composting, fermentation, acidification).

N.A.

v

Gas-permeable membranes (GPM) for ammonia capture from gaseous media

Microporous, hydrophobic membranes are used to capture ammonia from the air. The driving force for ammonia transfer across membranes is the concentration gradient. The process is carried out at atmospheric pressure. Once the ammonia is in the acidic solution (on the other side of the membrane), it combines with free protons to form non-volatile ammonium ions (NH4 +). Various inorganic or organic acids can be used to prepare the acidic solution, such as sulphuric, hydrochloric, phosphoric and lactic acid. This way, ammonia contained in the air is transformed into a valuable ammonium fertiliser (e.g. ammonium sulphate, ammonium chloride, ammonium phosphate, ammonium lactate), which can also be transported off site. This technique is suitable for both natural and forced ventilation in the buildings, as it operates independently of the ventilation system. The system is usually located outside the housing and needs sufficient space for maintenance.

Ammonia reductions of at least 75 % (in pig farms) and 45 % (in poultry farms) can be achieved in comparison to a reference system without the use of this technique.

An ammonia reduction of at least 45 % can be achieved in comparison to a “deep litter / pit with partial litter” reference system.

Drying of litter on manure belts (in the case of aviaries)

For non-ventilated manure belts, an ammonia reduction of at least 70 % can be achieved in comparison to a “deep litter / pit with partial litter” reference system.

For ventilated manure belts, an ammonia reduction of at least 80 % can be achieved in comparison to a “deep litter / pit with partial litter” reference system.

i

Forced air drying of litter on manure belt (in the case of aviaries)

The technique reduces ammonia emissions from manure through targeted airflow around it, which slows down microbial activity (of breaking down uric acid in poultry manure) and limits the conversion of nitrogen compounds into volatile ammonia. Manure falls onto belts located beneath the slats, where it is dried using air (e.g. 0,3 m3 of air per animal per hour at a minimum temperature of 20 °C) supplied through ducts under or beside the slatted floor. The airflow is strategically placed to ensure uniform drying, avoiding zones of high humidity. The system operates continuously, ensuring manure remains in a semi-dry state (e.g. dry matter content of at least 50 %) from the moment it is excreted until removal (at least once a week or twice per week if further drying is used).

An ammonia reduction of at least 62 % can be achieved in comparison to a “deep litter / pit with partial litter” reference system. Dust emissions are also reduced.

j

Forced drying of litter using indoor air (in the case of a solid floor with deep litter)

In a deep litter system without a manure pit, indoor air recirculation systems can be used to dry the litter, while meeting the physiological needs of the birds. To this end, fans, heat exchangers and/or heaters can be used.

An ammonia reduction of at least 25 % can be achieved in comparison to a “deep litter / pit with partial litter” reference system.

k

Litter slide (in the case of aviaries)

The technique keeps the thickness of the litter layer limited (e.g. maximum 3 cm) over the entire litter-covered floor surface, by frequently removing a portion of the litter layer with a slide.

An ammonia reduction of at least 15 % can be achieved in comparison to a “deep litter / pit with partial litter” reference system. Dust emissions are also reduced.

l

Forced drying of litter using indoor air (in the case of a solid floor with deep litter)

Indoor air recirculation systems can be used to dry the litter, while meeting the physiological needs of the birds.

m

Forced drying of litter using heated indoor air (in the case of a solid floor with deep litter)

Ammonia emissions are reduced by drying the bedding with warm air and removing evaporated moisture with heated air (achieved by heat exchangers and/or heaters). Two configurations are possible:

1.

Heating pipes are placed along the side wall inside the animal housing beneath the air inlet. The heated air from the pipes rises due to thermals and moves, together with the incoming air from the air inlets, towards the centre of the barn.

2.

The warm air from the pipes, placed beneath air supply ducts in the roof (positive pressure ventilation), is mixed with the incoming air from the supply ducts and is evenly distributed over the entire surface of the barn which ensures uniform drying of the bedding. The heated air can absorb moisture evaporating from the bedding. A portion of the barn air is extracted so that the evaporated moisture is removed directly from the barn. The pipe heating system cannot be used in a covered run.

An ammonia reduction of at least 55 % can be achieved in comparison to a “deep litter / pit with partial litter” reference system.

n

Natural or forced ventilation with a non-leaking drinking system (in the case of a solid floor with deep litter)

The solid floor is fully covered with litter which can be added to upon necessity. Floor insulation (using concrete, clay for example) prevents water condensation in the litter. Solid manure is removed at the end of the rearing cycle. The design and operation of the drinking water system prevents leakage and spillage of water on the litter. Additional structures considered as outdoor areas, such as exercise yards, verandas, free-range areas or winter gardens can be present. Natural ventilation can be combined with a free-range system.

N.A.

o

Heated and cooled littered floor (in the case of combideck systems)

A closed water circuit is installed below the floor and another is built at a deeper level for storing the excess heat or to return it to the poultry house when needed. A heat pump connects the two water circuits. At the beginning of the rearing period, the floor is heated with the stored heat in order to keep the litter dry by avoiding moisture condensation; during the second rearing cycle, birds produce an excess of heat which is preserved in the storing circuit while cooling down the floor, which reduces the breakdown of uric acid by reducing microbial activity.

An ammonia reduction of at least 40 % can be achieved in comparison to a “deep litter / pit with partial litter” reference system.

Litter composed of straw and sodium hydrogen sulphate

In order to reduce the pH value of litter, which in turn inhibits microbiological activity, a pellet made of a combination of 80 % straw (or other renewable material) and 20 % sodium hydrogen sulphate (NaHSO4) is used as bedding.

An ammonia reduction of at least 58 % can be achieved in comparison to a “deep litter / pit with partial litter” reference system.

(b)

Description

Information on emissions

a

Biofilter

The exhaust air is led through a filter bed of organic material, such as root wood or wood chips, coarse bark, compost or peat. The filter material is always kept moist by intermittent sprinkling of the surface. Odorous air compounds and dust particles and are absorbed by the wet film and are oxidised or degraded by microorganisms living on the moistened litter material. A sufficient area outside the animal house is needed to accommodate the filter packages.

Bioscrubber (or biotrickling filter)

A packed tower filter with inert packing material which is normally maintained continuously wet by sprinkling water. Air pollutants are absorbed in the liquid phase and subsequently degraded by microorganisms settling on the filter elements. The inoculation of selected microorganisms mixtures may enhance the efficiency of biofilters.

Partial air cleaning (e.g. to target a selected share of the exhaust air with a higher pollutant load) can also be applied in certain cases.

An ammonia reduction of at least 70 % can be achieved in comparison to a reference system without the use of this technique. Odour and dust emissions are also reduced.

c

Dry filter

The exhaust air is blown against a screen made of, for example, multi-layered plastic placed in front of the end wall ventilator. The passing air is subject to strong changes of direction, causing the separation of particles by centrifugal force, resulting in the reduction of dust emissions.

N.A.

d

Two-stage or three-stage air cleaning system

In a two-stage system, the first stage (wet acid scrubber) is usually combined with a bioscrubber (second stage). Partial air cleaning (e.g. to target a selected share of the exhaust air with a higher load of pollutants) can also be applied in certain cases.

Water scrubber

The exhaust air is blown through a packed filter medium by transverse flow. Water is continuously sprayed on the packing material. Dust is removed and settles in the water tank, which is emptied before refilling. This technique is also known as a “trickle bed reactor”.

Water trap

The exhaust air is directed by ventilation fans down onto a water bath where dust particles get soaked. The flow is then redirected 180 degrees upward. The water level is topped up regularly to compensate for evaporation.

N.A.

The exhaust air is forced through a filter (e.g. packed wall) where a circulating acid liquid (e.g. sulphuric acid) is sprayed. Partial air cleaning (e.g. to target a selected share of the exhaust air with a higher load of pollutants) can also be applied in certain cases.

An ammonia reduction of at least 70 % can be achieved in comparison to a reference system without the use of this technique. Odour and dust emissions are also reduced.

Description

Dust emissions

a

Prevention or reduction of dust generation inside livestock buildings by using, e.g., low-dust littering techniques, coarser litter material, litter slides, ad libitum feeding, low-dust feed, dust separators, optimised airspeed ventilation

Dust generation inside livestock building is prevented or reduced by applying techniques such as:

(a)

Application of fresh litter using a low-dust littering technique: littering techniques which release little dust are used, instead of techniques (e.g. blowers or mechanical distributors) that break up and disperse the litter;

(d)

(e)

Use of feed generating low dust: moist feed (e.g. soup or mash), pelleted feed or addition of oily raw materials or binders in dry feed systems are used, while ensuring a varied diet taking into account the proper functioning of the digestive system and the welfare of the animals;

(f)

Dust separators: dry feed stores which are filled pneumatically are equipped with dust separators, to capture dust that is generated during feed movement operations;

(g)

Optimised airspeed ventilation: the ventilation system is designed and operated to maintain a constantly optimised airspeed within the livestock housing environment. This prioritises consistent airflow patterns over short bursts of high-velocity ventilation (“purge cycles”), which may lead to rapid spikes in the airborne dust concentration.

Reduction of dust concentration inside livestock buildings by applying, e.g. water fogging, ionisation, oil spraying

The dust concentration inside livestock buildings is reduced by applying techniques such as:

(a)

(b)

Ionisation: an electrostatic field is created in the house. resulting in an electrical charge (negative or positive) to dust. This causes the particles to adhere to oppositely charged surfaces or earthed surfaces, where they can be removed;

(c)

Oil spraying: pure vegetable oil is sprayed by nozzles inside the house. A mixture of water and around 3 % vegetable oil can also be used for spraying. Circulating dust particles are bound to the oil drops and collected in the litter. A thin layer of vegetable oil is also applied on the litter to prevent dust emissions. Wet or moist litter needs to be avoided.

keeping the animals and the surfaces dry and clean (e.g. avoid feed spillages, avoid dung in lying areas with partly slatted floors);

decreasing the air flow and velocity over the manure surface;

The drinking water equipment is regularly checked and, if necessary, adjusted, to ensure consistent flow rates and functionality, while avoiding water spillages.

i

Energy consumption

High-efficiency heating/cooling and ventilation systems

High-efficiency heating/cooling and ventilation systems employ technical solutions minimising energy consumption, while taking into account animal welfare requirements.

k

Optimisation of heating/cooling and ventilation systems and management, especially where air cleaning systems are used

Existing heating/cooling and ventilation systems are improved, while taking into account animal welfare requirements (e.g. concentration of air pollutants, appropriate temperatures). This can be achieved by measures such as:

(a)

implementation of automation and smart sensors;

(b)

replacement for high-efficiency motors;

(c)

use of frequency converters (e.g. for fans) and electronically commutated motors;

(d)

correct distribution of heating/cooling and ventilation equipment, temperature sensors and separate heated areas.

l

Insulation of the walls, floors and/or ceilings of animal housing

Insulation material can be naturally impermeable or provided with an impermeable coating. Permeable materials are provided with a vapour barrier installed, as humidity is a major cause of insulation material deterioration.

m

Heat recovery (e.g. combideck system, heat recovery ventilation systems, slurry cooling heat recovery)

Recycling thermal energy from ventilation, waste products, or internal structures.

A central unit uses an air-to-air heat exchanger to transfer heat from warm, stale exhaust air to cold, fresh incoming air.

For the description of the combideck system, see Section 4.2.2.

n

Use of natural ventilation

Free (natural) ventilation in the animal house is generated by thermal effects and/or wind flow. The animal house can have openings in the ridge and, if necessary, also on the gable sides in addition to controllable openings in the side walls. The openings can be equipped with wind protection nets. Fan assistance can be used during hot weather.

o

Use of hybrid ventilation

Forced and natural ventilation are combined in different sections in the animal house. In the forced ventilated section, animals are protected from cold, heat, solar radiation, and rain. In the naturally ventilated section, animals have access to outdoor conditions (in the case of pigs, the excretion zone is typically located in the outdoor area).

p

Evaporative cooling systems using pads and water recirculation

Pad evaporative cooling systems use rigid cellulose, aspen or synthetic porous panels installed on one wall of the housing, continuously saturated with water by pumping. Water that is not evaporated or misted is collected and recirculated into the system. Tunnel fans draw outside air through the wet panels, evaporating water and cooling the air.

filter pressing.

Separation of liquid and solid fractions with different dry matter content, using for example screw press separators, decanter-centrifuge separators, separation by sieves or filter pressing. Separation can be enhanced by additives or by applying different temperatures/pressures.

The technique is applied, for example, when a reduction of the nitrogen and phosphorus content in the solid phase is needed due to limited available land for manure application.

b

Anaerobic digestion

Anaerobic microorganisms decompose the organic matter of manure in a closed reactor in the absence of oxygen. Biogas is produced and collected to serve energy generation, i.e. production of heat, combined heat and power, and/or transport fuel. Some of the heat produced is recycled in the process. The stabilised residue (digestate) can be used as fertiliser (with sufficiently solid digestate after composting). Solid manure can be co-digested with slurry and/or other co-substrates, while ensuring a dry matter content lower than 18 %. The inoculation of selected microorganisms mixtures may enhance the speed and efficiency of the process.

c

Manure drying with an external tunnel

Manure is collected from the laying hen houses and removed by belts that convey it outdoors to a dedicated closed structure, containing a series of perforated overlapping belts that form the tunnel. Warm air is blown through the belts, drying the manure in about two or three days. The tunnel is ventilated with air extracted from the laying hens' house.

d

Mechanical separation of slurry into solid and liquid fractions, followed by aerobic digestion (aeration) of the liquid fraction, with nutrient recovery

The slurry is separated into liquid and solid fractions using mechanical separation equipment and nutrients are recovered when the solid and liquid fractions are used for crop nutrition. Most of the phosphorus and a substantial portion of the nitrogen are retained in the solid fraction, which is a product with lower volume and weight.

The liquid fraction is aerated, intermittently and at low intensity, by means of submerged or floating aerators in a continuous or batch process which induces biological oxidation of volatile organic compounds. The process is managed through the control of operating parameters, such as aeration intensity (dissolved oxygen concentration), pH increase limitation, temperature (e.g. between 45 oC and 75 oC) and minimising slurry mixing, with the objective of decreasing ammonia (NH3) losses to the atmosphere, while suppressing anaerobic conditions and methane formation. Ammonia released during aeration can be optionally collected (see for example the technique “Gas-permeable membranes (GPM) for ammonia capture from gaseous media”). Foam and sludge, which contain some nutrients (e.g. P) captured from the liquid fraction, are collected from the reactor and dried using mechanical separators, and then managed with the rest of the solid fraction.

The technique is typically applied where pathogen and odour reduction is important prior to landspreading. Care should be taken to address possible cross-media effects.

e

Mechanical separation of slurry into solid and liquid fractions, followed by nitrification-denitrification of the liquid fraction, with nutrient recovery

The slurry is separated into liquid and solid fractions using mechanical separation equipment and nutrients are recovered when the solid and liquid fraction are used for crop nutrition. Most of the phosphorus and a substantial portion of the nitrogen are retained in the solid fraction, which is a product with lower volume and weight.

The liquid fraction is subjected to nitrification via biological treatment under controlled aerobic conditions, in which ammonia (NH3) is oxidised to nitrites (NO2 –) and nitrates (NO3 –), thus reducing ammonia losses to the atmosphere. Coagulants and flocculants are added to enhance the process. The effluent, rich in nitrates, and also containing any remaining P, as well as K, Na and Ca, may be reused in agriculture (e.g. through fertigation). Alternatively, it can be forwarded to the denitrification stage, in which nitrite and nitrate are transformed into N2 in the presence of organic carbon.

Some of the microorganisms can be recirculated to the aeration basin to maintain biological activity. Foam and sludge, which contain some nutrients (e.g. P) captured from the liquid fraction, are collected from the basin(s) and dried using mechanical separators, and then managed with the rest of the solid fraction.

The nitrification-denitrification process can be achieved in a single basin or multiple basins in series. Through the setting of the retention time in the basin(s), the degree of nitrification-denitrification, including the concentration of the nitrates in the effluent, can be controlled. Care should be taken to address possible cross-media effects.

f

Composting of solid manure

Solid manure, including the solid fraction of the slurry, is decomposed by microorganisms under aerobic conditions, producing a final product (compost) sufficiently stable for transport, storage and landspreading. Biological inoculum, straw, sawdust, green residues or other organic wastes (e.g. digestate) can be co-composted with solid manure. Odour, microbial pathogens and water content of manure are reduced. The supply of oxygen is achieved by mechanical reversal of the windrows or by forced aeration of the heaps. Drums and composting (vertical and horizontal) tanks/silos can also be used. The inoculation of selected microorganism mixtures may enhance the speed and efficiency of the process.

The technique can be used, for example, when pathogen and odour reduction is important prior to landspreading. Windrows need sufficient space to be established on the farm. Other composting options (e.g. tanks/silos, drums) usually require less space.

g

Vacuum evaporation with ammonia recovery

Evaporation is used to reduce digestate volume (by up to 70 %), resulting in concentrated nutrients. The liquid phase of the manure is typically evaporated under vacuum conditions at low temperatures (e.g. approximately 60 °C). During the evaporation process, gaseous ammonia is bound as an ammonium sulphate solution. Vacuum evaporation is primarily a method for reducing the volume of farm manure.

h

Vacuum fine separators with screens

The technique consists of a continuous fine separation technology designed for nutrient-optimised processing of liquid nutrient-rich suspensions (e.g. slurries, digestates). It enables solid-liquid separation into an energy-rich solid fraction and a nutrient-reduced liquid phase, suitable for further membrane filtration or fertiliser processing. This technique is suitable for liquids with low dry matter content (e.g. 1 %) using, for instance, fibre-retaining screens with different aperture sizes. It can be used in combination with pre-separation techniques.

i

Pressure and flocculation solid-liquid separators

A slurry treatment technique consisting of several stages. First, manure is divided into a solid and a liquid fraction. The liquid fraction is then subjected to a physico-chemical treatment complemented by decantation, which results in clarification and removal of suspended solids. Afterwards, the treated liquid is acidified to reduce ammonia emissions. In parallel, the solid fraction and sludge generated throughout the different stages of the liquid treatment undergo a dewatering process and are dried using solar energy.

An ammonia reduction of at least 63 % can be achieved in comparison to a reference system without the use of this technique.

j

Mechanical separation of slurry in a solid and liquid fraction, followed by treatment in constructed wetlands of the liquid fraction, with nutrient recovery.

The slurry is separated into liquid and solid fractions using mechanical separation equipment. If there is enough space, the separation may be further enhanced by a subsequent decantation step. Most of the phosphorus and a substantial portion of the nitrogen are retained in the solid fraction, which is a product with lower volume and weight.

The liquid fraction flows to the constructed wetland, where it percolates the subsurface through the gravel bed planted with specialised plant species (e.g. Phragmites australis, Juncus, Typha, Apium nodiflorum), without flooding the sand surface. Nitrogen (as NO3 – and NH4 +) is absorbed by the plants, thus reducing ammonia (NH3) losses to the atmosphere. Possible emissions of nitrous oxide (N2O) are kept low with proper management of the operational parameters. Suspended solids and Biological Oxygen Demand (BOD) are also reduced. The treated effluent exits the constructed wetland by gravity into a collection pond through a drainage pipe and can be reused in agriculture (e.g. through fertigation), since it contains any remaining N and/or P. No energy consumption is needed for the operation of the constructed wetlands.

The system is adequately sized (hydraulic retention time is 3-5 days) to handle the whole volume of the liquid fraction and is designed to prevent any leakage to the environment (i.e. soil and groundwater), by incorporating double-layer plastic impermeabilisation. Periodic harvesting of vegetation/biomass, which can be used as co-substrate for anaerobic digestion or as feedstock for composting, and removal of the accumulated organic matter are done as part of proper management. Care should be taken to address possible cross-media effects.

f

Slurry application through band spreaders, using one of the following techniques:

(a)

trailing hose;

(b)

trailing shoe.

Band spreading is a precision application method delivering slurry in narrow bands rather than uniform broadcasting.

A trailing hose consists of a series of flexible hoses hanging from a wide bar mounted onto the slurry trailer. The hoses discharge slurry at ground level in wide parallel bands, enabling application between the rows of arable crops.

A trailing shoe consists of a series of rigid pipes which terminate in metal ‘shoes’, designed to apply slurry directly in narrow bands to the soil surface and below the crop canopy. Some types of trailing shoes are designed to cut a shallow slit in the soil to aid infiltration.

Trailing hose: An ammonia reduction of at least 30 % can be achieved in comparison to spreading over the whole soil surface (“broadcast”) without incorporation.

Trailing shoe: An ammonia reduction of at least 40 % can be achieved in comparison to spreading over the whole soil surface (“broadcast”) without incorporation.

g

Slurry application through shallow injectors (open slot)

An injector using tines, knives or discs to cut vertical slots (typically 4 to6 cm deep) in the soil, forming grooves into which slurry is deposited. The injected slurry is fully or partially placed below the soil surface and grooves will normally be left open after slurry application.

An ammonia reduction of at least 60 % can be achieved in comparison to spreading over the whole soil surface (“broadcast”) without incorporation.

h

Slurry application through deep injectors (closed slot)

An injector using tines, knives or discs to cultivate the soil and deposit slurry into it, before covering the slurry fully by means of press wheels or rollers. The depth of the closed slot ranges between 10 cm and 20 cm.

Ammonia reductions of at least 80 % (for slots < 15 cm) and 90 % (for slots ≥ 15 cm) can be achieved in comparison to spreading over the whole soil surface (“broadcast”) without incorporation.

i

Manure incorporation in the soil as soon as possible after application (with a time delay up to four hours or up to 12 hours when conditions are not favourable for a faster incorporation)

Manure incorporation involves mixing or covering surface-applied manure into the soil to minimise nutrient losses. This is done by different tillage practices (e.g. cultivators, disc harrows) depending on the soil type and conditions. Manure is completely mixed with soil or buried.

Incorporation is either immediate or with a maximum time delay up to four hours. The time delay can be up to 12 hours when conditions are not favourable for a faster incorporation (i.e. when human and machinery resources are not available and/or in case of adverse climate conditions).

Incorporation takes place after spreading via a suitable spreader (e.g. rota-spreader, rear discharge spreader, dual-purpose spreader for solid manure, band spreader for slurry).

Ammonia reductions of at least 90 % (for immediate slurry incorporation by primary tillage practices), 70 % (for immediate slurry incorporation by non-inversion cultivation), 65 % (for immediate solid manure incorporation by non-inversion cultivation), 55 % (for slurry incorporation in less than 4 hours), 50 % (for solid manure incorporation in less than 4 hours), 45 % (for slurry incorporation between 4 and 12 hours), 35 % (for solid manure incorporation between 4 and 12 hours), 30 % (for slurry incorporation beyond 12 hours) and 25 % (for solid manure incorporation beyond 12 hours) can be achieved in comparison to spreading over the whole soil surface (“broadcast”) without incorporation.

4.8.2.

 Monitoring of ammonia emissions to air

 

Technique

Description

a

Mass balance based on the excretion and the total (or total ammoniacal) nitrogen present at each manure management stage

Ammonia emissions are estimated based on the amount of nitrogen excreted by each animal category and using the total nitrogen (or the total ammoniacal nitrogen – TAN) flow and the volatilisation coefficients (VC) over each manure management stage (housing, storage, landspreading).

The equations applied for each of the manure management stages are:

Ehousing = Nexcreted · VChousing

Estorage = Nstorage · VCstorage

Espreading = Nspreading · VCspreading

Eprocessing = Nprocessing · VCprocessing

where:

(a)

E is the annual NH3 emission from the animal house, manure storage or landspreading (e.g. in kg NH3/animal place/year);

(b)

N is the annual total nitrogen or TAN excreted, stored or applied in landspreading (e.g. in kg N/animal place/year). If appropriate, nitrogen additions (e.g. related to litter, recycling of scrubbing liquids) and/or nitrogen losses (e.g. related to manure processing) can be considered;

(c)

VC is the volatilisation coefficient (dimensionless, related to the housing system, manure storage or landspreading techniques) representing the proportion of TAN or Total nitrogen emitted to air.

VC are derived from measurements designed and performed according to a national or an international protocol (e.g. VERA protocol) and validated for a farm with an identical type of technique and similar climatic conditions. Alternatively, information to derive VC can be taken from European or other internationally recognised guidance. The mass balance considers in particular any significant change to the type of livestock reared at the farm and/or to the techniques applied for housing, storage and landspreading.

The monitoring technique is further developed at Member State level. In order to apply the monitoring technique, farms need to keep record of information on key parameters (according to the methodology defined by the Member State), e.g. numbers of animals, feed intake, crude protein content in the diet, quantity of manure generated, on-farm manure management practices.

b

Emission factors

Ammonia and dust emissions can be estimated on the basis of emission factors derived from measurements designed and performed according to a national or an international protocol (e.g. VERA protocol) on a farm with an identical type of technique (related to the housing system, manure storage and/or landspreading) and similar climatic conditions.

Additionally, there are international guidance documents (e.g. the EMEP/EEA emission inventory guidebook) provididing a tiered approach for the calculation of ammonia emissions at national level. This approach consists of three possible methods (Tier 1, Tier 2 and Tier 3) with increasing levels of complexity and accuracy. The Tier selection depends on the availability of data, significance of the livestock sector in the region, and the level of detail required.

Tier 1 - This approach is a simple method that uses a single default emission factor (EF) to estimate ammonia emissions from particular animal categories. The EF is multiplied by the average annual population of each animal category to calculate emissions. The default Tier 1 EFs are calculated using Tier 2 default NH3-N EFs, default N excretion data, and default data on proportions of total ammoniacal nitrogen (TAN) in excreta.

Tier 2 - This approach uses mass flow to estimate ammonia emissions from livestock. It is based on the concept of a flow of total ammoniacal nitrogen (TAN) through the manure management system, which is composed of animal housing systems or holding areas, manure storage, and field application of manure. This approach requires more input data than Tier 1, including country-specific activity data, N excretion rates, and proportions of TAN in manure. Tier 2 estimates the amount of TAN available at each stage of manure management and calculates emissions using a mass balance approach. This approach is more accurate than Tier 1 and can account for differences in management systems and resulting emissions. It also allows for the inclusion of abatement measures and can be used to estimate emissions from multiple livestock categories.

Tier 3 - This approach is an advanced method that uses emission modelling and installation data to estimate ammonia emissions from livestock. It is based on the use of country-specific emission factors (EFs) or the inclusion of abatement measures. Tier 3 can utilise the calculation procedure outlined under Tier 2, but with the use of country-specific EFs or the inclusion of abatement measures. This approach requires a high level of detail and accuracy, including data on facility-specific emissions, abatement measures, and management practices. It must be well documented and accompanied by supporting literature to ensure transparency and accuracy and can provide highly accurate estimates of ammonia emissions from livestock.

For the purposes of ammonia emissions monitoring, a monitoring technique based on Tier 3 is to be developed at Member State level. In order to apply the monitoring technique, farms need to keep record of information on key parameters (according to the methodology defined by the Member State), e.g. numbers of animals, feed intake, crude protein content in the diet, quantity of manure generated, on-farm manure management practices.

5.   EMERGING TECHNIQUES

5.1.   Ammonia and methane emissions from animal houses

 

Technique

Description

Information on emissions

a

Adsorption of ammonia from gaseous media

Acidic, porous adsorbents (e.g. clays modified with metal oxides) are used to remove ammonia from the air and slurry. For air treatment, adsorbents are installed in ventilation columns, where ammonia, from the air which passes through the column, binds to the adsorbent via chemisorption or physisorption. Some adsorbents allow regeneration via thermal or vacuum desorption, enabling their reuse, although their cost may be higher and farm energy consumption may be increased. For slurry treatment, adsorbents can be directly added to pits or storage facilities, without the need for any specific modification to the housing structure (e.g. installation of an air ventilation system with columns) and without additional energy consumption for their reuse. However, since these non-regenerable materials stay in the solid slurry phase, they must not contain any toxic substances.

An ammonia reduction of at least 38 % is expected to be achieved in comparison to a reference system without the use of this technique.

b

Alternative litter materials

Alternative litter materials are used, with favourable characteristics in terms of prevention of ammonia generation and the consequent decrease in emissions in comparison to conventional litter materials (e.g. straw, pellets, wood shavings). The emission-reducing properties can be enhanced by the use of additives in conventional materials. By changing the chemical or microbial characteristics of the litter, the processes responsible for ammonia generation can be slowed down. The choice of litter material and its additives needs to take into account their subsequent use or disposal.

d

Robot for removing slurry

Slurry vacuum robots are autonomous devices operating under the slatted floors in pig houses. They aim to remove slurry continuously, thus decreasing ammonia emissions and odour, as well as providing better comfort for the pigs and farm employees. The frequency of robots’ passages can be adjusted (e.g. several times a day). In order to implement the technique in existing farms, a minimum clearance under the grating is needed (e.g. more than 80 cm), the floor slope needs to be limited (e.g. less than 10 %), and drilling through walls between rooms must be possible.

An ammonia reduction of at least 28 % is expected to be achieved in comparison to a reference system of deep pit with fully slatted floor without any additional techniques. Methane emissions are also expected to be reduced.

Technique

Description

a

Flaring of methane from slurry storage

Methane-rich gases collected from covered slurry stores are diverted to a low-pressure flare that oxidises methane to carbon dioxide and water vapour, thereby significantly reducing the climate impact compared to direct atmospheric release of methane. The technique requires slurry tanks to be equipped with systems such as dense tent covers, in order to avoid air in-leaks and maintain the methane concentration above 5 %. The gases are extracted using a vacuum pump, then pass through a condensate trap (the captured condensates are pumped back to the slurry storage) before reaching the burner, while the combustion air is added just below the burner head. Instruments such as spark igniters, as well as flame, temperature and excess oxygen sensors, are used to adjust the process to optimal combustion conditions. The energy in the combustion process can be partially recovered using heat exchangers, if a demand exists on farm. This technique does not require the use of additional fuel for pilot flame.

Technique

Description

a

Phosphorus recovery from manure

Phosphate salts are precipitated through the addition of ions (e.g. magnesium and potassium) and a pH regulator, often in a reactor. To optimise the process and minimise unwanted co-precipitation, it is necessary to monitor the solution’s pH and the molar ratios of the ions present in thesolution during the process. Phosphorus can be recovered in the form of salts (e.g. struvite) which can be reused as fertilisers.

b

Resource recovery from digestates by using gypsum

Digestate produced on farm is treated to obtain mineral fertilisers by using gypsum, which can be “natural” or “synthetic”, i.e. generated in flue-gas desulphurisation systems (gas cleaning systems used for capturing sulphur dioxide emissions from flue-gases). When gypsum is added to digestate, ammonia and carbon dioxide are gradually stripped at low temperatures/pressures (e.g. 50 to 85°C / 200 to 700 mbar, without air or steam). Precise adjustment of the chemical and thermodynamic equilibria in each step ensures low power and heat consumption and a highly efficient process. In order to decrease the power consumption, waste heat from another process can be used. The formed suspension is separated using a filter press into two mineral fertilisers: ammonium sulphate (solution) and calcium carbonate (solid).

c

Gas-permeable membranes (GPM) for ammonia capture from liquid media

This technique operates in two steps: in the first step, gaseous ammonia is released from ammonia-rich liquid streams (e.g. anaerobic digestate, slurry) by aeration and spraying, generating an ammonia-rich air stream; in the second step, this gas stream comes into contact with a microporous, hydrophobic membrane, where ammonia passes through the pores of the membrane. The driving force for ammonia transfer across membranes is the concentration gradient. The process is carried out at atmospheric pressure. Once the ammonia is in the acidic solution (on the other side of the membrane), it combines with free protons to form non-volatile ammonium ions (NH4 +). Various inorganic or organic acids can be used to prepare the acidic solution, such as sulphuric, hydrochloric, phosphoric and lactic acid. In this way, ammonia contained in the liquid is transformed into a valuable ammonium fertiliser (e.g. ammonium sulphate, ammonium chloride, ammonium phosphate, ammonium lactate). The system is usually located outside the housing and needs sufficient space for maintenance.

An ammonia reduction of at least 30 % is expected to be achieved in comparison to a reference system without the use of this technique.
