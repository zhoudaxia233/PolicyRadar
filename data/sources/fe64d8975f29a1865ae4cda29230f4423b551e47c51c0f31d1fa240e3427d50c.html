    STATE AID – ROMANIA

    State aid SA. 113263 (2024/NN) – Romania – Arbitration award to LSG Building Solutions GmbH and others (ICSID Case No. ARB/18/19)

    Invitation to submit comments pursuant to Article 108(2) of the Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union

    (Text with EEA relevance)

    (C/2026/5197)

    By means of the letter dated 29 July 2026 reproduced in the authentic language on the pages following this summary, the Commission notified Romania of its decision to initiate the procedure laid down in Article 108(2) of the Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union concerning the abovementioned measure.

    Interested parties may submit their comments on the measure in respect of which the Commission is initiating the procedure within one month of the date of publication of this summary and the following letter, to:

    European Commission,

    Directorate-General Competition

    State Aid Greffe

    1049 Bruxelles/Brussel

    BELGIQUE/BELGIË

    Stateaidgreffe@ec.europa.eu

    These comments will be communicated to Romania. Confidential treatment of the identity of the interested party submitting the comments and/or of parts of the comments submitted may be requested in writing, stating the reasons for the request.

    SUMMARY OF THE DECISION

    On 19 March 2024, Romania notified to the Commission the arbitration award rendered on 20 February 2024 by the Arbitration Tribunal established under the auspices of the International Centre for Settlement of Investment Disputes (‘ICSID’), in the arbitration proceedings LSG Building Solutions GbmH and others v. Romania (ICSID Case No. ARB/18/19). This arbitration award ordered Romania to pay compensation of EUR 42.2 million to the claimants, together with interest and a contribution towards the costs of the arbitration proceedings, due to Romania’s breach of the Energy Charter Treaty (the ‘ECT’) (the ‘Award’).

    The claimants in the arbitration proceedings are 10 project developers who invested in five solar photovoltaic (‘PV’) plants in southern Romania between 2011 and 2013: LSG Building Solutions GmbH, Green Source Consulting GmbH, Core Value Investments GmbH & Co. KG Gamma and Core Value Capital GmbH, which are incorporated under the laws of Austria; Anina Pro Invest Ltd. and Giust Ltd., which are incorporated under the laws of Cyprus; Risen Energy Solar Project GmbH and Pressburg UK GmbH, which are incorporated under the laws of Germany; Solluce Romania 1 B.V., which is incorporated under the laws of the Netherlands; and SC LJG Green Source Energy BETA S.R.L, which is incorporated under the laws of Romania (together, the ‘Claimants’).

    Romania implemented a green certificates support scheme under Law 220/2008 to encourage investments in renewable energy production. The Claimants benefitted from the green certificates support scheme.

    The Commission approved the green certificates support scheme as compatible aid with the internal market in its decision of 13 July 2011 (SA.33134). Romania amended its green certificates support scheme over the years. The Commission has further assessed and approved the amendments of that support scheme in the decisions of 4 May 2015 (SA.37177) and of 16 December 2016 (SA.46894).

    In the arbitration proceedings that the Claimants initiated against Romania, they argued that the amendments to the green certificates support scheme enacted by Romania had a devastating impact on the value of their investments in Romania, in violation of Romania’s obligations under the ECT. The Arbitration Tribunal concluded that Romania breached Article 10(1) of the ECT with respect to the Claimants’ investments and awarded them compensation by determining what would have happened if Romania had not breached its obligations under the ECT and had not amended the green certificates support scheme.

    Romania notified the Award to the Commission on 19 March 2024. On 31 May 2024, in order to suspend any enforcement proceedings that could be initiated by the Claimants, Romania made a payment into a special-purpose account opened in the name of the Claimants in the amount of RON 256 020 536,28 (ca. EUR 51 million). The amount will remain deposited in that account until the Commission adopts a decision on the compatibility of the relevant State aid with the internal market.

    The Commission considers, at this stage, that the cumulative criteria for the existence of State aid are met and that the measure, namely the Award and in any event, its implementation, constitutes State aid.

    Since the aid under the Award was granted on 20 February 2024 before being approved by the Commission, the Commission considers that the aid resulting from the Award is unlawful and has been put into effect in violation of Article 108(3) TFEU.

    Romania has not presented arguments that could justify the compatibility of the measure under Articles 107(2) and/or 107(3) of the TFEU. At this stage of the procedure, the Commission expresses its preliminary doubts about the compatibility of the measure with the internal market, and in particular about a possible breach of EU Treaties by the aid measure. Given that the Award was adopted based on the investor-State arbitration mechanism laid down in Article 26 of the ECT, the Commission, at this stage, takes the view that the award could contravene Article 19(1) TEU, Articles 267 and 344 TFEU, and the general principles of EU law of mutual trust and autonomy.

    In accordance with Article 16 of Council Regulation (EU) 2015/1589, all unlawful aid can be subject to recovery from the recipients.

    TEXT OF LETTER

    The Commission wishes to inform Romania that, having examined the information supplied by your authorities on the aid/measure referred to above, it has decided to initiate the procedure laid down in Article 108(2) of the Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union (‘TFEU’).

    1.   Procedure:

    (1)

    		 On 19 March 2024, Romania notified to the Commission, pursuant to Article 108(3) TFEU, the compensation due under the arbitration award rendered on 20 February 2024 by the Arbitration Tribunal (the ‘Tribunal’), established under the auspices of the International Centre for Settlement of Investment Disputes (‘ICSID’), in the arbitration proceedings LSG Building Solutions GmbH and others v. Romania (1). The Tribunal found a breach of the Energy Charter Treaty (the ‘ECT’) by Romania in the Decision on Jurisdiction, Liability and Principles of Reparation of 11 July 2022 (2), and reserved its decision on damages in these proceedings (3). The award rendered by the Tribunal on 20 February 2024 in conclusion of those proceedings orders Romania to pay compensation to ten claimants established in Austria, Cyprus, Germany, and The Netherlands, together with interest and a contribution towards the costs of the arbitration proceedings, due to Romania’s breach of the ECT (4) (the ‘Award’).

    (2)

    		 The Commission requested additional information from Romania on 14 June 2024.

    (3)

    		 On 17 June 2024, Romania submitted further information in this case.

    (4)

    		 A videoconference between the Commission services and the Romanian authorities took place on 27 August 2024. Romania provided additional information on 3 September 2024.

    (5)

    		 On 27 June 2025, Romania submitted responses to the Commission’s request of 14 June 2024.

    (6)

    		 Another meeting between the Commission services and the Romanian authorities took place on 16 February 2026. Romania provided additional information on 19 February 2026.

    (7)

    		 The Commission requested further additional information on 22 April 2026, which Romania provided on 4 June 2026. The Commission requested additional information on 4 June 2026, which Romania provided on 3 July 2026.

    (8)

    		 On 4 June 2026, Romania exceptionally agreed to waive its rights deriving from Article 342 TFEU, in conjunction with Article 3 of Council Regulation No 1/1958 (5), and to have this Decision adopted and notified in English.

    2.   Background in connection with intra-EU arbitration

    (9)

    		 As consistently considered by the Commission (6) and held by the Court of Justice of the European Union (‘Court of Justice’ or ‘Court’) (7), intra-EU investor-State arbitration mechanisms arising from international treaties such as Bilateral Investment Treaties (‘BITs’) and the ECT are contrary to EU law, and in particular to Article 19(1) of the Treaty on the European Union (‘TEU’), Articles 267 and 344 TFEU and the principle of autonomy of the EU legal order.

    (10)

    		 On 6 March 2018, the Court of Justice held in its Achmea judgment that investor-State arbitration clauses in intra-EU BITs are incompatible with the EU Treaties, and in particular with Articles 267 and 344 TFEU, because such clauses have an adverse effect on the autonomy of Union law (8).

    (11)

    		 The reasoning set out in the Achmea judgment is equally applicable to the intra-EU application of the ECT as stated by the Court of Justice in the Komstroy judgment (9). In this judgment, the Court of Justice held that a provision such as Article 26 ECT is intended to govern bilateral relations between two of the Contracting Parties to the ECT, in an analogous way to the provision of the BIT at issue in the case giving rise to the Achmea judgment (10). The Court added that intra-EU arbitration proceedings initiated in accordance with Article 26 ECT would remove from the jurisdiction of Member States’ courts, and hence, from the system of judicial remedies which the second subparagraph of Article 19(1) TEU requires them to establish in the fields covered by Union law, disputes which may concern the application or interpretation of that law (11). The Court found that Article 26(2), point (c), of the ECT must therefore be interpreted as not being applicable to disputes between a Member State and an investor of another Member State concerning an investment made by the latter in the first Member State (12).

    (12)

    		 In the Romatsa order, the Court of Justice further held that an arbitral award delivered in proceedings between a Member State and an investor from another Member State, on the basis of an arbitral clause provided for in an international agreement, is incompatible with EU law, in particular with Articles 267 and 344 TFEU, and therefore cannot produce any effect, and cannot be executed (13). In such a case, a court of a Member State may not in any case proceed with the enforcement of such an award in order to enable its beneficiaries to obtain the payment of damages under such an award (14).

    (13)

    		 The Commission and the Member States took several actions to implement or otherwise ensure the effectiveness of the judgments of the Court of Justice.

    (14)

    		 On 15 January 2019, Member States signed a declaration on the consequences of the judgment of the Court of Justice in Achmea and on investment protection in the European Union (15).

    (15)

    		 On 5 May 2020, 23 Member States signed an agreement for the termination of intra-EU BITs. This agreement entered into force on 29 August 2020 (16). The other Member States terminated their intra-EU BITs bilaterally, so that all intra-EU BITs have been formally removed from the legal order (17).

    (16)

    		 On 26 June 2024, the EU and Member States signed a declaration on the legal consequences of the Komstroy judgment and common understanding on the non-applicability of Article 26 ECT as a basis for intra-EU arbitration proceedings (18). They also closed negotiations on the text of a formal international agreement between themselves designed to put an end to the continuation of intra-EU arbitration proceedings that are contrary to Union law (19). In particular, the declaration and the agreement clarify, for the benefit of courts and arbitral tribunals, that the arbitration clause provided in the ECT does not apply – and has never applied – to relations between an EU investor and another Member State.

    (17)

    		 On 27 June 2024, the Union and Euratom notified to the depositary of the ECT their respective withdrawals from the ECT (20), since it was found that in the absence of any substantial modifications, the ECT is no longer compatible with the principles of the Paris Agreement, the requirements of sustainable development and the fight against climate change, as well as with modern standards of investment protection. In addition, as explained in the explanatory memorandum of the Commission's proposal for the decision of withdrawal, the ECT is incompatible with the principle of autonomy of Union law, as it does not include some of the safeguards identified by the Court of Justice in the CETA opinion (21) in order to conclude that the arbitration awards would not have the ‘effect of preventing the EU institutions from operating in accordance with the EU constitutional framework’ (22). These withdrawals took effect on 28 June 2025.

    (18)

    		 On 10 September 2025, the EU approved an inter se agreement signed by all but one EU Member States, expressing their view that the ECT’s investor-state arbitration clause ‘cannot and never could’ serve as a legal basis for intra-EU investment arbitration (23). The international agreement was published in the Official Journal of the EU on 27 March 2026 (24).

    3.   Background on the Romanian renewables support measures

    (19)

    		 In this section, the Commission provides, by way of background, a short description of the Romanian green certificates support scheme that led to the arbitration proceedings and, finally, the Award.

    3.1.   The Romanian green certificates support scheme

    (20)

    		 The Romanian support scheme for renewable energy is based on the one hand, on green certificates (‘GC’) that are granted by the State, free of charge, to producers of electricity from renewable energy sources (‘RES electricity’) and, on the other hand, on an obligation imposed on electricity suppliers to acquire a certain number of GCs (‘GC support scheme’). The GC support scheme was designed to assist Romania in reaching by 2020 the mandatory national renewable energy target set under EU legislation. The beneficiaries are producers of RES electricity from the following renewable energy sources: hydropower <10 MW, wind, solar photovoltaic (‘PV’), biomass, landfill gas and sewage treatment plants. The GC support scheme was open to new beneficiaries until 31 December 2016. The GC support scheme provided that the beneficiaries would receive a certain number of GCs (depending on technology) for each MWh of electricity produced from renewable energy sources, for a period of 15 years. The GC support scheme budget was initially estimated by Romania at EUR 19.5 billion. The support in the form of GCs could be cumulated with investment aid – in such case, the number of GCs would be reduced accordingly.

    (21)

    		 The national legal basis of the GC support scheme is the Romanian Law 220/2008, which was enacted on 27 October 2008. The key elements of the GC support scheme under Law 220/2008 were as follows:

    (a)

    an annual mandatory quota corresponding to the share of RES electricity that could benefit from the GC support scheme (‘Annual Mandatory Quota’) (25);

    (b)

    an annual mandatory quota for the acquisition by electricity suppliers of a defined number of GCs (‘Annual Mandatory Acquisition Quota’); and

    (c)

    obligations of the Romanian National Energy Regulatory Authority (‘ANRE’) with respect to the operation of the GC support scheme, including, inter alia, the setting of the Annual Mandatory Quota on a yearly basis (26), oversight of electricity suppliers on the fulfilment of their Annual Mandatory Acquisition Quotas, imposition of penalties on electricity suppliers for failure to meet the Annual Mandatory Acquisition Quota (27), and the setting of the minimum and maximum trading value of the GCs.

    3.2.   The 2011 Commission Decision in SA.33134

    (22)

    		 On 6 June 2011, Romania notified the GC support scheme to the Commission (Case SA.33134 (28)). The legal basis for this scheme was Law 220/2008, as modified by Law 139/2010 and by a draft Government Emergency Ordinance (29) notified by the Romanian authorities on 6 June 2011 to the Commission.

    (23)

    		 On 13 July 2011, the Commission adopted a decision approving the GC support scheme (‘2011 Commission Decision’) (30).

    (24)

    		 The Commission found the GC support scheme compliant with the 2008 Community Guidelines on State Aid for Environmental Protection concerning operating aid for renewable energy sources (31) and therefore compatible with the internal market in accordance with Article 107(3), point c) TFEU.

    (25)

    		 In particular, in order to avoid overcompensation, the Romanian authorities confirmed that the production costs and revenues (market price of electricity, revenues from GCs) would be monitored on a yearly basis, and that the support levels for new beneficiaries would be adapted in case a risk of overcompensation was identified. Given this commitment of the Romanian authorities to adapt the notified measure in time in order to avoid overcompensation, the Commission found that the notified measure was in line with the condition of absence of overcompensation (32).

    (26)

    		 Romania implemented this commitment by means of adopting the Emergency Government Ordinance 88/2011 (‘EGO 88/2011’) in October 2011. In its notification, Romania had submitted this ordinance in draft form, which formed part of the legal basis of the notified scheme and therefore of the Commission’s assessment, in particular on the questions of proportionality and risk of overcompensation. EGO 88/2011 provided that ANRE would monitor the costs and revenues of RES electricity producers for overcompensation and, if needed, would propose measures to reduce the number of GCs allocated to RES electricity producers. Such measures would apply only to RES electricity producers who begin producing electricity after the measures were adopted (33).

    3.3.   2011 - 2014 Amendments

    (27)

    		 After the Commission adopted the 2011 Commission Decision, the Romanian authorities issued a number of regulations amending the GC support scheme regarding, among other things, the accreditation of RES electricity producers, the issuance of GCs and the organisation of the GC market.

    (28)

    		 In particular, Law 134/2012 enacted by the Romanian Parliament in July 2012, approved EGO 88/2011 and further envisaged the possibility for Romania to reduce the number of GCs for solar PV plants accredited after 1 January 2014, in case of overcompensation (34).

    (29)

    		 On 29 March 2013, ANRE published a report on the overcompensation analysis of the GC support scheme for the year 2012, concluding that there was an overcompensation in the number of GCs for wind, solar PV and small hydro installations. ANRE estimated that RES electricity producers from solar PV were being overcompensated by obtaining three GCs per MWh in excess (35).

    (30)

    		 The Romanian Government subsequently introduced a number of further modifications to the GC support scheme, inter alia with the following amendments to Law 220/2008:

    (a)

    EGO 57/2013 adopted on 4 June 2013, deferred the issuance of two of the six GCs per MWh for solar PV plants between 1 July 2013 and 31 March 2017, to decrease the expected GC revenue of solar PV plants (36). This measure would apply to any solar PV plant accredited by ANRE after the enactment of EGO 57/2013 (i.e. after 4 June 2013) (37).

    (b)

    For solar PV plants with a capacity above 5 MW, EGO 57/2013 prohibited the issuance of GCs for electricity beyond the projection that RES electricity producers made to the grid operator in the day-ahead hourly forecast (38). This meant that if RES electricity producers’ actual production was higher than predicted, they would receive only the projected number of GCs, and not the number of GCs based on actual production.

    (c)

    EGO 57/2013 restricted the trading of GCs to the centralised market (39) thereby excluding the potential for trading on the basis of bilateral GC purchase agreements foreseen by Law 220/2008.

    (d)

    Law 23/2014 enacted by the Romanian Parliament on 14 March 2014, inter alia, approved EGO 57/2013 and reduced the validity of GCs from 16 (40) to 12 months from their date of issuance (41).

    3.4.   The 2015 Commission Decision

    (31)

    		 On 20 March 2014, Romania notified to the Commission several amendments to the GC support scheme introduced by, inter alia, Law 134/2012, EGO 57/2013, EGO 79/2013, and Law 23/2014 (Case SA.37177). Romania confirmed that these amendments did not alter the functioning of the GC support scheme but only modified the amount and intensity of support for some beneficiaries (42). Romania estimated that the respective amendments would lead to a budget reduction (from EUR 19.5 billion to EUR 17.4 billion) (43).

    (32)

    		 The Commission also received several complaints and submissions from third parties concerning the respective amendments adopted by the Romanian authorities (44).

    (33)

    		 On 4 May 2015, the Commission adopted a decision finding the GC support scheme, as amended, compatible with the internal market in accordance with Article 107(3), point c) TFEU (‘2015 Commission Decision’) (45).

    (34)

    		 In the 2015 Commission Decision, the Commission concluded that the GC support scheme constituted State aid within the meaning of Article 107(1) TFEU. As Romania had already implemented some of the notified amendments, without waiting for the Commission’s decision, the Commission had registered the case as a non-notified aid procedure and concluded that Romania had put the aid measure into effect in breach of Article 108(3) TFEU (46).

    (35)

    		 In the 2015 Commission Decision, the Commission stated that the notified amendments may have the effect of reducing support for RES electricity producers for certain technologies (new hydro, wind and solar PV). Part of GCs were suspended and could not be traded before April 2017 (for new hydro and solar PV) or 2018 (for wind). The Commission noted that this might postpone certain income flows for the beneficiaries, negatively affecting their profitability. In addition, no GCs would be granted for electricity delivered to the grid in addition to the hourly quantities indicated by the RES electricity producers to the TSO in advance, which might reduce the revenue of beneficiaries, in particular for intermittent technologies (e.g. wind and solar PV). The Commission also held that the reduction of the Annual Mandatory Acquisition Quota could also create a reduction in the demand for GCs, which might as a consequence decrease the price of GCs (47).

    (36)

    		 The Commission stated in the 2015 Commission Decision that it had already concluded in the 2011 Decision, that the GC support scheme was compatible with the internal market, and that the amendments made to the GC support scheme did not change this conclusion. These amendments can be considered as the tightening of the criteria for granting the aid, whose effect is a reduction of the aid amount and intensity of the aid, leading to a lower rate of return for the beneficiaries, which further decreases the risk of overcompensation (48).

    (37)

    		 The Commission noted in the 2015 Commission Decision that Member States are under no obligation to provide State aid and therefore, they are also under no obligation to provide sufficient aid so as to ensure a minimum level of support to the beneficiaries. Under certain circumstances, different forms of aid can be cumulated, as long as they do not lead to overcompensation. This shows that sometimes a certain aid measure implemented by a Member State may not be sufficient to achieve the desired effects. The Commission also noted that the GC support scheme, under the GC support scheme approved in 2011 and subsequently amended by the measures approved by the Commission in 2015 and 2016, did not provide guaranteed minimum revenues for the beneficiaries (49).

    (38)

    		 Therefore, the Commission concluded in the 2015 Commission Decision that the amendments introduced by the Romanian authorities did not lead to overcompensation. The Commission considered that the aid measure pursued an objective of common interest in a necessary and proportionate way, the distortions of competition were limited, and therefore the aid was compatible with the internal market on the basis of the Guidelines on State aid for environmental protection and energy 2014-2020 (50) (‘EEAG’) until the expiry of the scheme on 31 December 2016.

    3.5.   Subsequent Amendments

    (39)

    		 Romania introduced further amendments to the GC support scheme, aimed at the continued operation of the scheme for already accredited installations, after the scheme was closed to new beneficiaries on 31 December 2016, including the following:

    (a)

    Increased period of validity of GCs: the validity of GCs was increased until the end of the support scheme (i.e. until 31 December 2031) from the previously applicable 12-month validity from the date of issuance;

    (b)

    Prolongation of the period of postponement and of the period of recovery of postponed GCs: the postponement period for solar PV plants was extended until 31 December 2024 (from 31 March 2017 under the approved GC support scheme). The recovery period for GCs for solar PV was postponed to the period from 1 January 2025 to 31 December 2030, in equal monthly instalments (instead of the period from 1 January 2018 to 30 December 2020 under the approved GC support scheme);

    (c)

    Modifications to the bookkeeping system: GCs would be allocated a value only upon their trading, and not from the moment they are received (as was the case under the approved GC support scheme);

    (d)

    Restrictions to GC trading: GCs to be traded exclusively on a centralised anonymous GC market (no prolongation of existing or creation of new bilateral GC purchase agreements);

    (e)

    Amendments to the minimum and maximum price for GCs: for the period from 1 January 2017 to 31 December 2031, the minimum GC price was increased from EUR 27 to EUR 29.40, and the maximum price was decreased from EUR 55 to EUR 35. As from 2017, the value of green certificates would no longer be indexed.

    (40)

    		 Romania implemented these amendments through EGO 24/2017, which was adopted on 30 March 2017 and entered into force on 31 March 2017, following its notification to the Commission in 2016 as explained below.

    (41)

    5.   Assessment of the measure

    5.1.   Existence of aid

    (95)

    		 Article 107(1) TFEU provides that ‘aid granted by a Member State or through State resources in any form whatsoever which distorts or threatens to distort competition by favouring certain undertakings or the production of certain goods shall, in so far as it affects trade between Member States, be incompatible with the internal market’.

    (96)

    		 A measure constitutes State aid within the meaning of Article 107(1) TFEU, if it fulfils four cumulative conditions. First, the measure must be imputable to the State and financed through State resources. Second, the measure must confer an advantage to a beneficiary. Third, the measure must favour certain undertakings or economic activities (that is, the advantage must be selective). Fourth, the measure must have the potential to affect trade between Member States and to distort, or threaten to distort, competition in the internal market.

    (97)

    		 The Commission will assess whether the Award, and in any event, the implementation, payment or execution of the Award (‘Implementation of the Award’) (together, the ‘Measure’), constitutes State aid within the meaning of Article 107(1) TFEU.

    5.1.1.   Imputability and State resources

    Imputability

    (98)

    		 According to the case-law of the Court of Justice, for a selective advantage to constitute aid within the meaning of Article 107(1) TFEU, it must, inter alia, be imputable to the State (109). It must also be noted that, where an advantage is granted by a public authority, that advantage is, by definition, attributable to the State (110).

    (99)

    		 The Commission considers that the Award can be considered as imputable to Romania because it results from Romania’s voluntary sovereign decision to sign and ratify the ECT and the ICSID Convention and to incorporate these instruments into its domestic legal order (recital (89)). Those treaties became part of national law in accordance with the Romanian Constitution (recital (90)).

    (100)

    		 The Tribunal rendered the Award on the basis of its interpretation of the ECT and the ICSID Convention that Romania voluntarily signed. The issuance of the Award has therefore been rendered possible by Romania’s decision to sign and ratify the ECT and the ICSID Convention.

    (101)

    		 In addition, the Commission considers that the Implementation of the Award is also to be considered as imputable to Romania.

    (102)

    		 An arbitral award delivered in proceedings between a Member State and an investor from another Member State, such as in the present case, is incompatible with Union law (in particular Articles 267 and 344 TFEU, Article 19 TEU and the general principle of autonomy) and therefore cannot produce any effect and cannot be executed (111). For these reasons, the General Court also held in Micula that the State is necessarily ‘involved’ if it pays the compensation provided for in an arbitral award (112).

    (103)

    		 Because the Award was delivered in proceedings between Romania and investors from other Member States, it cannot produce any effect in the EU legal order and cannot be executed. Romania is required to set aside the Award and cannot be considered as being under an obligation to pay, implement or execute it (113).

    (104)

    		 The Implementation of the Award in spite of that prohibition directly involves an autonomous decision of the Romanian authorities and would be also imputable to the Romanian State. That holds true if Romania voluntarily pays out the sums awarded, and if Romania would attempt initially to oppose the recognition and execution of the Award before a court of a Member State or a third country, and either fails to prevent seizure of assets or takes the decision to pay the Award in order to prevent measures of asset discovery and ultimately seizure of assets not protected by sovereign immunity (114). The Commission also notes, for the sake of completeness, that the recognition or execution of the Award by a Romanian court would equally be attributable to Romania as the acts of all State organs, including the national courts or bailiffs, are imputable to Romania.

    (105)

    		 As explained in recital (83), Romania decided to voluntarily pay out the compensation awarded by the Tribunal to an account in the name of the beneficiaries. This is an action imputable to the Romanian State.

    State resources

    (106)

    		 According to settled case-law, only advantages granted directly or indirectly through State resources can constitute State aid within the meaning of Article 107(1) TFEU (115).

    (107)

    		 In this case, the Award contains an obligation for Romania to pay compensation of EUR 42.2 million plus interest for Romania’s breach of the ECT (recital (77)). The Romanian authorities explained that payment of the Award would be financed by State resources because it would be financed from the general budget of the Romanian State and paid by the Romanian Ministry of Finance, (recital (93)). Moreover, the payment made by Romania to the blocked account in the name of the Claimants was paid by the Ministry of Finance (see recital (83)(93)), so this payment was also financed through State resources.

    (108)

    		 Finally, there is further risk that Romania will have to invest State resources in the payment of the Award as a result of the Implementation of the Award through execution. Such risk suffices for the finding of the presence of State resources (116). In that context, the Court of Justice has held that it is irrelevant for the finding of State resources whether such use of State resources would violate Union law or national law. What is decisive is that the measure has produced effects (117). As the measure entails a risk of direct payment by Romania, from its budget, the Commission considers that the Award and, in any event, the Implementation of the Award, which would consist of a direct payment, is financed by State resources.

    Conclusion on imputability and State resources

    (109)

    		 Based on the foregoing, the Commission preliminarily considers that the Award and, in any event the Implementation of the Award can be considered as imputable to the Romanian State and financed through State resources within the meaning of Article 107(1) TFEU.

    5.1.2.

    		 Advantage and selectivity

    Advantage

    (110)

    		 An advantage, within the meaning of Article 107(1) TFEU, is any economic benefit which an undertaking would not have obtained under normal market conditions, that is to say in the absence of the State intervention (118). The precise form of the measure is irrelevant in establishing whether it confers an economic advantage on the undertaking (119).

    (111)

    		 In that regard, in order to assess whether a Member State has conferred an advantage on a given undertaking, the financial situation of the undertaking following the measure should be compared with its financial situation if the measure had not been taken (120). There is therefore an advantage where, as a result of the measure and without that being justified by the nature or general scheme of the system concerned, the net financial situation of the beneficiary is improved (121).

    (112)

    		 Moreover, the concept of ‘advantage’, which is intrinsic to the classification of a measure as State aid, is an objective one, irrespective of the motives of the persons responsible for the measure in question. Accordingly, the nature of the objectives pursued by State measures and their grounds of justification have no bearing whatsoever on whether such measures are to be classified as State aid. Article 107(1) TFEU does not distinguish between the causes or the objectives of State aid, but defines them in relation to their effects (122).

    (113)

    		 In the present case, the Commission takes the preliminary view that the Measure confers an advantage to the Claimants for the following reasons.

    (114)

    		 Firstly, the Measure improves the financial situation of the Claimants compared to their financial situation without it. The Tribunal awarded the Claimants an amount of EUR 42.2 million as compensation plus interests. The Award has a monetary value and could be transferred to other parties against suitable payment, as seen by the trade of arbitration awards in other cases (123). The Claimants now hold the Award and the right of financial value under it, which they would not have obtained under normal market conditions.

    (115)

    		 Under normal market conditions, i.e. without the Award handed down by an arbitration tribunal on the basis of Romania’s signature of the ECT and the ICSID Convention, the Claimants would not have been entitled to any compensation such as that obtained as a result of the Award, and there would be no title on the basis of which to seek implementation (124). The Claimants had already received aid under the GC support scheme to the extent that was available under the applicable national laws in Romania. The Award has the effect of going beyond what an undertaking could have obtained under the applicable national law. The Award therefore confers a right which other investors in the same situation would not have obtained following a lawful procedure in Romania.

    (116)

    		 Secondly, for the reasons set out below (recitals (117) - (118)) the Measure confers an economic advantage because it has the effect of reinstating amounts that the Claimants claimed under the GC support scheme, which were not available to them after the modifications to the GC support scheme introduced by Romania in line with the 2011 Commission decision. The Commission also considers that the Award does not merely compensate them for any unlawful action by Romania for the reasons also set out in recital (118).

    (117)

    		 In accordance with the case-law of the Union Courts, where national legislation has established ‘State aid’ within the meaning of Article 107(1) TFEU, the payment of a sum claimed before the courts in accordance with that legislation also constitutes such aid (125). While the case-law considers that a distinction must be drawn between claims for compensation for damage resulting from unlawfulness of State action and claims for the payment of amounts duly due under national legislation, where sums claimed before the courts, even formally as compensation, correspond to the payment of an advantage which the applicant is seeking pursuant to legislation, the action does not seek compensation for harm distinct from that consisting of the complete non-payment of the advantage to which the applicant considered he or she was entitled under that legislation (126). The recipient of aid cannot circumvent the effective application of the rules on State aid by obtaining, without relying on Union law on State aid, a judgment granting compensation whose effect would enable it, definitively, to continue to implement the aid in question over a number of years (127).

    (118)

    		 In this case, firstly, as explained in recital (78), the Tribunal considered that the breach of the ECT resulted from the modifications of the GC support scheme, and awarded compensation to the Claimants for losses resulting directly from the modifications of the GC support scheme by Romania. The compensation awarded to the Claimants by virtue of the Award was calculated as the difference in economic value that the Claimants would have received if Romania had not amended the GC support scheme and the GC support scheme continued to apply unaltered over the years (recitals (78) - (79)). The Award therefore has the effect of re-establishing the situation the Claimants would have found themselves in, if the GC support scheme continued to apply unaltered. The GC support scheme constituted State aid within the meaning of Article 107(1) TFEU, as already concluded by the Commission in the 2015 Commission Decision and the 2016 Commission Decision, and some amendments were also unlawful as implemented before the approval by the Commission (see the 2015 Commission Decision and recital (34)). Romania amended the scheme in view of market developments and the conditions of competition on the market for the GCs, in accordance with its commitment made in the context of the 2011 Commission to monitor the scheme and ensure that this would not result in overcompensation (recitals (25) - (26)). The Commission has thus approved amendments to the GC support scheme on the premise that that the amendments introduced by the Romanian authorities did not lead to overcompensation (recital (38)). Therefore, any attempt to compensate the Claimants for the consequences of the modification of the GC support scheme grants an advantage that goes beyond what the beneficiaries of the GC support scheme would have obtained and would not be available under those normal market conditions.

    (119)

    		 In addition, although the Tribunal found an infringement of the fair and equitable treatment clause under Article 10 ECT, the Award did not constitute compensation from unlawful action of the State. As explained in recital (118), the Measure constitutes compensation for the modification of the GC supports scheme and aims to re-establish the situation the Claimants would have found themselves in, if the GC support scheme continued to apply unaltered. Therefore, the Measure does not compensate for Romania’s unlawful action. In addition, as the Court of Justice held in the Anie judgment, Article 10 ECT only applies in relation to investors from ‘other’ contracting parties, which excludes EU investors (128). Therefore, Article 10 ECT was not applicable in the present case because the present case concerns a dispute between a Member States and EU investors of other Member States.

    (120)

    		 Finally, in any event, the Measure has not been granted under normal market conditions because the Award is contrary to Union law for the reasons explained in Section 5.3.1. The Award exists and as such has never been repealed. The Commission thus considers that the Award remains liable to have an impact if its implementation takes place.

    (121)

    		 In view of the above, the Commission considers that the Measure constitutes an economic advantage in favour of the Claimants that the Claimants would not have obtained under normal market conditions.

    Economic advantage granted to an undertaking

    (122)

    		 The Court of Justice has consistently defined undertakings as entities engaged in an economic activity, regardless of their legal status and the way in which they are financed (129). The classification of a particular entity as an undertaking thus depends entirely on the nature of its activities and more specifically whether these are economic. Moreover, the Court of Justice has consistently held that any activity consisting in offering goods and services on a market is an economic activity (130).

    (123)

    		 Separate legal entities may be considered to form one economic unit for the purpose of the application of State aid rules. That economic unit is then considered to be the relevant undertaking. To that end, it is necessary to take into consideration the existence of a controlling share and the existence of other functional, economic and organic links (131). The Court of Justice has indicated the factors to be taken into account to determine the absence or existence of an economic unit (132).

    (124)

    		 The Claimants are companies established in the EU and active in several sectors in the EU. They also invested in the renewable energy sector in Romania and own and operate PV plants in Romania (section 4.1). On this basis, they appear to offer goods and services on a market and therefore engage in an economic activity. The Commission therefore takes the preliminary view that the Claimants are undertakings within the meaning of Article 107(1) TFEU.

    Selectivity

    (125)

    		 To fall within the scope of Article 107(1) TFEU, a State measure must favour ‘certain undertakings or the production of certain goods’. Hence, not all measures that grant an undertaking an economic advantage fall under the notion of State aid, but only those which confer an economic advantage in a selective way upon certain undertakings or categories of undertakings or to certain economic sectors.

    (126)

    		 According to settled case-law, where an individual aid is at issue, the identification of the economic advantage is, in principle, sufficient to support the presumption that a measure is selective. This is so, regardless of whether there are operators on the relevant markets that are in a comparable situation (133).

    (127)

    		 In this case, the Award is an individual aid constituting a separate legal act that grants a right to compensation only to the Claimants (see recitals (1) and (48)). For completeness, the Commission notes that Romania also transferred the amount to an account with the State Treasury opened in the name of the Claimants and the amount will remain deposited in that account until the Commission adopts a final decision (recital (83)).

    (128)

    		 The Award and in any event the Implementation of the Award is selective, since it is awarded specifically to the Claimants, while other undertakings in a comparable legal and factual situation within that sector or other sectors are not eligible for aid and thus will not receive the same advantage.

    Conclusion on advantage and selectivity

    (129)

    		 Based on the above, the Commission preliminarily considers that the Award and, in any event, the Implementation of the Award, confers a selective advantage to the Claimants within the meaning of Article 107(1) TFEU.

    5.1.3.   Distortion of competition and effect on trade

    (130)

    		 A measure granted by the State is considered to distort or threaten to distort competition when it is liable to improve the competitive position of the recipient compared to other undertakings with which it competes (134). A distortion of competition within the meaning of Article 107 TFEU is thus assumed as soon as the State grants a financial advantage to an undertaking in a liberalised sector where there is, or could be, competition (135). An advantage granted to an undertaking that distorts competition will be liable to affect trade between Member States where it strengthens the competitive position of the beneficiary undertaking as compared with other undertakings competing in intra-Union trade (136).

    (131)

    		 In this case, the beneficiaries of the Measure are the Claimants, which are considered as undertakings and exercise economic activity consisting of investing in infrastructure and energy in the Union (section 4.1). The energy and investment sectors are liberalised sectors where there is intra-EU competition. Thus, the advantage granted to the Claimants is liable to distort competition and to strengthen their competitive position as compared with other undertakings competing in intra-Union trade.

    (132)

    		 Consequently, the Commission preliminarily considers that the Measure is liable to affect trade between Member States and to distort, or threaten to distort, competition in the internal market.

    5.1.4.   Conclusion on existence of aid

    (133)

    		 In view of the above, the Commission preliminarily considers that the notified Measure constitutes State aid under the meaning of Article 107(1) TFEU.

    5.2.   Lawfulness of the aid

    (134)

    		 Article 108(3) TFEU requires that the Member State concerned shall not put its proposed measure into effect until the Commission procedure has resulted in a final decision.

    (135)

    		 According to well-established case-law, the decisive factor for establishing the date on which the right to receive State aid was conferred on its beneficiaries by a particular measure is the acquisition by those beneficiaries of a definitive right to receive that aid and to the corresponding commitment, by the State, to grant that aid. It is at that date that such a measure is liable to distort competition and affect trade between Member States, within the meaning of Article 107(1) TFEU (137).

    (136)

    		 In this case, the Tribunal delivered the Award on 20 February 2024 and ordered Romania to pay compensation plus interests to the Claimants. The Claimants were conferred the right to receive the compensation ordered in the Award on that date (20 February 2024). Based on the Tribunal’s finding that the ECT applied to the dispute at hand, Article 26, paragraph (8) ECT means that Romania is bound by the outcome of the arbitration proceedings and the right for the Claimants to receive the compensation under the Award was therefore conferred on 20 February 2024 and the aid was thus granted under the Award on this same date.

    (137)

    		 Romania notified the Award on 19 March 2024. On 31 May 2024, Romania made a payment to an account opened in the name of the Claimants and the amount will remain deposited in that account until the Commission adopts a final decision (recital (83)).

    (138)

    		 Therefore, the Commission preliminarily considers that the aid resulting from the Award and its Implementation is unlawful and has been put into effect in violation of Article 108(3) TFEU. The Commission also considers that any further payments for the Implementation of the Award after the adoption of this Decision would also constitute unlawful aid put into effect in violation of Article 108(3) of the Treaty.

    5.3.   Compatibility of aid

    (139)

    		 Given that the Award, and in any event the Implementation of the Award, constitutes State aid within the meaning of Article 107(1) TFEU, such measure shall be prohibited unless it can be deemed compatible with the internal market pursuant to Article 107(2) or Article 107(3) TFEU.

    (140)

    		 At the outset, the Commission recalls that when assessing the compatibility of a measure with the internal market under Articles 107(2) and 107(3) TFEU, the burden of proof is the principal responsibility of the Member State (138). In this case, Romania has not presented any arguments that could justify the [compatibility of the] Measure under Article 107(2) or Article 107(3) TFEU.

    (141)

    		 In any event, the Commission has doubts as to whether the measure could be compatible with the internal market in so far as, for the reasons set out below, it preliminarily considers that the Measure breaches Union law.

    5.3.1.   Breach of Union law

    (142)

    		 According to settled case-law of the Court of Justice, ‘State aid which, as such or by reason of some modalities thereof, contravenes provisions or general principles of EU law cannot be declared compatible with the internal market […]. Indeed, where the modalities of an aid measure are so indissolubly linked to the object of the aid that it is impossible to evaluate them separately, their effect on the compatibility or incompatibility of the aid viewed as a whole must therefore of necessity be determined in the light of the procedure prescribed in Article 108 TFEU’ (139).

    (143)

    		 At the outset, the Commission recalls that the Union courts consider that an international agreement cannot affect the allocation of powers fixed by the Treaties or, consequently, the autonomy of the Union legal system, observance of which is ensured by the Court (140). That principle is enshrined in particular in Article 344 TFEU, by virtue of which the Member States undertake not to submit a dispute concerning the interpretation or application of the Treaties to any method of dispute settlement other than those provided for in the Treaties (141).

    (144)

    		 In order to ensure that the specific characteristics and the autonomy of the EU legal order are preserved, the Treaties have established a judicial system intended to ensure consistency and uniformity in the interpretation of Union law (142). In that context, in accordance with Article 19(1) TEU, it is for the national courts and tribunals and the Court of Justice to ensure the full application of Union law in all Member States and to ensure judicial protection of the rights of individuals under that law (143). The judicial system as thus conceived has as its keystone the preliminary ruling procedure provided for in Article 267 TFEU, which, by setting up a dialogue between one court and another, and specifically between the Court of Justice and the courts and tribunals of the Member States, has the object of securing uniform interpretation of EU law, thereby serving to ensure its consistency, its full effect and its autonomy as well as, ultimately, the particular nature of the law established by the Treaties (144).

    (145)

    		 As explained in recitals (9) - (18), according to the case-law of the Court of Justice, Article 19 TEU and Articles 267 and 344 TFEU as well as the general principle of the autonomy of the EU legal order preclude a provision contained in an international agreement under which an investor of one Member States party to that agreement may, in the event of a dispute concerning investments in another Member State party to that agreement, bring proceedings against the latter Member State before an arbitral tribunal, the jurisdiction of which that Member State has undertaken to accept (145).

    (146)

    		 Indeed, by concluding such an agreement, the Member States party to it consent to remove from the jurisdiction of their own courts and, therefore, from the system of judicial remedies which the second subparagraph of Article 19(1) TEU requires them to establish in the fields covered by Union law disputes which may concern the application or interpretation of Union law. Such an agreement is therefore liable to lead to a situation in which those disputes are not settled in a manner which guarantees the full effectiveness of Union law (146).

    (147)

    		 In this case, the Award concerns a dispute between a Member State (namely, Romania) and investors of other Member States (namely of Austria, Cyprus, Germany, and the Netherlands) and was adopted in the context of proceedings initiated on the basis of the investor-State arbitration mechanism laid down in Article 26 of the ECT.

    (148)

    		 As held by the Court of Justice in the Komstroy judgment, the ECT is, by virtue of the act approving its conclusion by the Union and in accordance with Article 216 TFEU, part of the Union legal order such that the ECT itself is an act of Union law (147). Therefore, any tribunal constituted under the ECT would be required to interpret, and even apply, Union law (148).

    (149)

    		 Moreover, as stated by the Court in the Komstroy judgment, an ad hoc tribunal, such as the Tribunal that rendered the Award, is not part of the judicial system of a Member State. It therefore means that it cannot be classified as a court or tribunal of a Member State within the meaning of Article 267 TFEU and is not therefore entitled to make a reference to the Court of Justice seeking a preliminary ruling on the interpretation of Union law (149). As a result of the incompatibility with Union law that would ensue if an investor from one Member State was able to trigger that arbitration mechanism in a dispute concerning an investment made by it in another Member State, the Court of Justice found that Article 26 ECT must be interpreted as not applying to such a situation, i.e. an intra-EU dispute.

    (150)

    		 Therefore, based on the above, the proceedings leading to the handing down of the Award lacked a legal basis. Article 26 ECT is not applicable to a dispute such as the one at hand between a Member State and investors of other Member States concerning an investment made by the latter in the first Member State. Romania never made any offer to arbitrate such disputes. There is therefore no agreement to arbitrate, and a tribunal established on the basis of a provision that does not apply is not properly constituted. The Tribunal that was nonetheless established in this case suffers from the defects identified by the Court in Komstroy. It was constituted under Article 37(2)(b) of the ICSID Convention which made the ICSID Convention applicable to the judicial review of its decisions, including the Award. Articles 52, 53 and 54 of the ICSID Convention provide only for limited review of the decisions concerning, in particular, the jurisdiction of the Tribunal. In this context, it must be considered that the Award rendered by the Tribunal is not subject to review by a court of a Member State capable of ensuring full compliance with Union law.

    (151)

    		 Therefore, even setting aside the fact that it is null because lacking a legal basis, the Award must be regarded as incompatible with Union law. It cannot have any effect and cannot be enforced in order to receive payment of the compensation awarded (150).

    (152)

    		 In view of the above, at this stage of the procedure, the Commission preliminarily considers that the Award, and, in any event, the Implementation of the Award, breach Union law, and in particular Article 19(1) TEU, and Articles 267 and 344 TFEU, as well as the general principle of the autonomy of the EU legal order.

    5.3.2.   Non-application of Article 351 TFEU

    (153)

    		 Article 351 TFEU provides that ‘[t]he rights and obligations arising from agreements concluded […] for acceding States, before the date of their accession, between one or more Member States on the one hand, and one or more third countries on the other, shall not be affected by the provisions of the Treaties’.

    (154)

    		 According to settled case-law, the purpose of the first paragraph of Article 351 TFEU is to make clear, in accordance with the principles of international law, that the application of the EU Treaties does not affect the duty of the Member State concerned to respect the rights of non-member countries under a prior agreement and to perform its obligations thereunder (151). The first paragraph of Article 351 TFEU has, therefore, the aim of protecting the rights of third countries by permitting the Member States concerned to perform their obligations under a prior international agreement (152). On the other hand, it does not authorise the Member States to exercise rights under such agreements in their internal relations within the Union (153).

    (155)

    		 The case-law further considers that, for a rule of Union law to be deprived of effect as a result of an international agreement, pursuant to the first paragraph of Article 351 TFEU, two conditions must be fulfilled: the agreement must have been concluded before the entry into force of the EU Treaties in the Member State concerned and the third State concerned must derive from it rights which it can require that Member State to respect (154).

    (156)

    		 In this case, as explained in footnote (2), Romania, Germany, Austria, The Netherlands and Cyprus ratified the ECT on 10 March 1996, 14 March 1997, 12 August 1997, 11 December 1997 and 2 January 1998, respectively. As also noted in recital (89), the ICSID Convention entered into force for Romania on 12 October 1975, for The Netherlands on 14 October 1966, for Cyprus on 25 December 1966, for Germany on 18 May 1969, and for Austria on 24 June 1971. Romania joined the EU in 2007, Austria on 1 January 1995 and Cyprus on 1 May 2004, while Germany and The Netherlands were already Member States as founding members of the European Communities. Article 26 ECT is, according to the Komstroy judgment, intended to govern bilateral relations (155). In the present case, the bilateral relations are between EU Member States (156). The second condition laid down in Article 351 TFEU is therefore not met by either the ECT or the ICSID Convention.

    (157)

    		 As a result, the application of State aid law in the present case does not affect rights and obligation protected under Article 351 of the TFEU.

    In the light of the foregoing considerations, the Commission, acting under the procedure laid down in Article 108(2) of the TFEU, requests Romania to submit its comments and to provide all such information as may help to assess the Measure, within one month of the date of receipt of this letter. It requests your authorities to forward a copy of this letter to the potential recipients of the aid immediately.

    The Commission wishes to remind Romania that Article 108(3) of the TFEU has suspensory effect and would draw Romania’s attention to Article 16 of Council Regulation (EU) 2015/1589, which provides that all unlawful aid may be recovered from the recipient.

    In this case, Romania shall ensure that no further payment, implementation or execution of the Award is otherwise effected from the date of adoption of this Decision.

    In order to comply with its obligations under Article 108(3) TFEU, Romania shall also take all appropriate measures to prevent the Claimants, and any third party that has acquired or may acquire the Award, or any right thereunder, from seeking recognition, enforcement and execution of the Award, whether in Member States of the Union or in third countries.

    Moreover, it shall be noted that it follows from the principle of sincere cooperation laid down in Article 4(3) TEU that the Member States, and in particular the national courts, have to take all measures that are necessary to guarantee the application and effectiveness of EU law, and in particular the rules of Union law on State aid laid down in Articles 107 and 108 TFEU (157). As a result of this same principle of sincere cooperation, national courts have to take all measures that are necessary to guarantee the application and effectiveness of Article 19(1) TEU, Articles 267 and 344 TFEU, and the principle of autonomy of the EU legal order (158). Therefore, in this case, the principle of sincere cooperation requires national courts of the Member States, including non-Romanian national courts, to not recognise, execute, or implement the Award, and to take all measures necessary to prevent the recognition, execution, or implementation of the Award (for example, by ordering undertakings established under their jurisdiction to cease any action designed to seek recognition or execution of the Award in other jurisdictions, and ordering them to pay penalty payments in case of non-compliance with the order to cease any such action).
